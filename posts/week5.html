Do the people who talk to each other also sound alike?

We pointed all of it at nine seasons of The Office, every line of dialogue tagged with who said it, and wired the speakers into a network of who answers whom. Then we asked one question: are conversation partners also linguistic neighbours?

Who talks to whom vs. who sounds like whom

An edge is a conversational turn: one character speaks right after another in the same episode. To say whether two people “sound alike” we compare their words in two separate ways, because every sentence is made of two very different kinds of word.

Before the chart · stopwords vs. content words
…
content word stopword spoken filler (also removed)

Content words = what you talk about

What is left after stopword removal: the nouns, verbs and adjectives that carry the topic, like beets, paper, Scranton, wedding, chili. They change with the subject of the conversation. (We also drop spoken filler such as yeah, okay, oh, which the written-English stopword list doesn’t know about.)

How we compare them: count each character’s content words and weight them with TF-IDF, so a word only a few characters use (beets) counts a lot and a word everyone uses counts little. Then take the cosine similarity of the two characters’ vectors.

High score = the two talk about the same things. In the buttons below this is called shared topics.

Stopwords = how you talk

The words a stopword list removes, also called function words: the small glue that holds every sentence together, like the, I, you, of, and, is, that, it. We use the 179 words of NLTK’s English stopword list. They carry almost no topic, but they make up about half of everything said in the show.

How we compare them: here we keep only the stopwords. We take the share of each one in a character’s speech (how often I vs. we, the vs. a), then cosine similarity again. Authorship studies use exactly this signal to tell writers apart, because people use the glue without thinking about it.

High score = the two build their sentences the same way. In the buttons below this is called shared style.

Where this comes from in week 5. Section 2 defines stopwords as the very frequent function words and says removing them leaves the content words. We use that split twice: once with the stopwords removed, once with only the stopwords. Sections 4–5 turn each character into a bag-of-words vector and compare vectors with cosine similarity. One tool is borrowed a week early: TF-IDF is previewed in Section 6 as the fix for “the and and dominate every comparison”. Our function-word result is that exact problem, measured: compare characters on the glue words alone and everyone looks almost identical.

      Methods and limitations

      TO:Corporate (and the 02805 teaching team) FROM:Scranton branch · C. Colberg, Y. Y. Hui, S. Jonsson RE:Methods, data, and what this can’t tell you

      Data. Fan transcripts of The Office (US) as packaged by the schrute project, re-parsed from its raw file (the tidy table cuts lines at colons); IMDb ratings from TidyTuesday 2020-03-17. Rebuild everything with python tools/build_week5_analysis.py.

      Method. Lines are lowercased and split into word tokens (apostrophes kept, so that’s stays one token); bracketed stage directions are removed. The talk network links two characters each time one speaks directly after the other in the same episode. “Lift” divides that count by what random mixing of everyone’s turns would predict. Similarity is cosine similarity between bag-of-words vectors from 30 random 2,000-token samples per character: TF-IDF over content words, or relative frequencies of the 179 NLTK stopwords for style. Significance comes from a Mantel-style test: shuffle the character labels on one matrix 1,999 times and count how often the shuffled Spearman ρ is at least as large as the real one.

      Limitations.

      • No scene breaks. “Spoke right after” is a stand-in for conversation. Cuts between talking heads and scene changes add some false edges.
      • Shared topics may partly be shared storylines. Controlling for how many episodes two characters share leaves a partial ρ of …, so it isn’t only that. But people in the same subplot talk about the same things, and we can’t fully separate that from “sounding alike”.
      • Small n. 27 characters give 351 pairs, but the pairs aren’t independent (each character appears in 26 of them). That’s why the test shuffles whole characters, not single pairs.
      • Fan transcripts. Spellings vary (“Bears. Beets.” is written several ways), some speaker names have typos (we merged the obvious ones), and nothing was checked against the aired audio.
      • Ratings are confounded by era. See Performance Reviews: the obvious result fell apart under one control.
      • The bot is deliberately simple. Plain bag-of-words cosine is this week’s method. A Naive Bayes model we tried did only slightly better (about 21%): sitcom lines are short, and most of their words are shared by everyone.
