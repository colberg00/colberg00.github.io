Case No. 02 · Open

Built, Not Born

Marvel's wiki-link network has hubs — a handful of characters everyone points to. Were they always going to end up that way, or does that structure have to be built, link by link, by a specific recipe? The Agency's new Field Toolkit lets you grow three candidate recipes from scratch and watch, live, which one actually manufactures a hub.

Research question

A network doesn't have to be born with hubs — it can grow into having them, or not, depending on two separate ingredients: whether it grows at all (new nodes arriving over time, vs. all nodes existing from the start), and whether new links prefer already-popular targets. Which combination of those two ingredients, grown to the same size as the real Marvel network, actually reproduces something that looks like it — a heavy-tailed degree distribution with a real hub at the top — and which combinations obviously can't?

Method, in one paragraph

The toolkit below grows two networks node-by-node to Marvel's exact roster size (303 files): one using preferential attachment (growth + a bias toward already-popular files, à la Barabási–Albert), and one using uniform growth (the same growth process, but new links land on a uniformly random existing file). A third, static random network (Erdős–Rényi) skips growth entirely — all 303 files exist from frame one, and links are assigned independently at a fixed probability. All three target the same number of links as the real network, so the comparison below is apples-to-apples on everything except the ingredient being tested.

Case Analysis I — Random, Barabási–Albert, or neither?

The single log–log slope estimated above is a start, not a verdict — 303 points is a small sample to hang a power-law exponent on, and one straight line hides whether that slope actually holds steady anywhere. This panel checks the harder way: it computes Marvel's degree CCDF (the complementary cumulative distribution, P(K ≥ k) — unlike a binned histogram it needs no bin width and is defined at every k), then tracks how the local slope of that curve moves as k increases. Alongside Marvel's own curve, the chart overlays two closed-form fits matched to Marvel's own size and average degree — a Poisson approximation to the Erdős–Rényi (random-graph) degree distribution, and the exact mean-field Barabási–Albert formula — so the comparison isn't just "run a synthetic network and eyeball it," it's a real statistical fit. Barabási–Albert theory predicts a local CCDF slope that holds flat near −2 over a wide range; a random graph with no heavy tail falls off a cliff almost immediately instead.

Case Analysis II — The friendship paradox

On average, your friends have more friends than you do — not because you picked unpopular friends, but because popular characters simply show up in more people's friend lists than they'd otherwise deserve. It's a pure sampling effect, and it should show up in Marvel's wiki-link network too. This panel computes it exactly (a closed-form ratio over the degree sequence, no sampling needed for that part), then draws two distributions side by side — the degree of a uniformly random character, and the degree of that character's own randomly chosen friend — so the size-biased shift is visible directly, not just as a single ratio. It also asks two follow-ups: who are the specific popular characters driving the effect, and is there anyone in the network who is never the less-popular one in a connection — a character nobody out-popularity-ies?

Case Analysis III — Chain of custody: what survives a shuffle?

Marvel's clustering coefficient is high — but so is its degree sequence's variance, and a few hub characters alone can inflate a summary statistic without any real "wiring" effect underneath. The only way to tell a wiring fact from a degree-sequence fact is to destroy the wiring while keeping the degree sequence exactly fixed, and see what's left standing. Pick a variable below, then run twenty degree-preserving double-edge-swap shuffles — the same links, reattached between random pairs of endpoints so every character keeps precisely as many connections as before — and see where the real network's value falls against that null distribution. Reciprocity uses the underlying directed wiki-links instead (article A links to B, independent of whether B links back), shuffled with the directed equivalent, since reciprocation is inherently about direction.

Discussion

The toolkit's real punchline is negative evidence: uniform growth and static random both fail to produce anything resembling Marvel's hub, even matched exactly on nodes and links. That rules two ingredients in as necessary — growth and preference together — without yet proving they're sufficient — that's exactly the gap Case Analysis III's shuffle test above closes: matching a real network on a couple of summary statistics is a much weaker claim than matching its generative process, and clustering there turned out not to survive being decoupled from Marvel's actual wiring.

Everything above runs client-side, generated fresh every time you hit EXECUTE — re-run any recipe as many times as you like, the randomness is real. Filed under the same investigation unit as Case No. 01, whose TSVs this case file borrows for its ground truth.
