Conflicting Testimony

The Agency's beat moves for a week from Wikipedia's Marvel characters to its philosophers — 1,444 of them, born anywhere from the centuries BC to the 19th century, wired together by 11,135 wiki-links. Ask this network who its factions are and you get four different answers depending on who you ask: two community-detection methods that only partly agree, one philosopher two different weighting schemes can't agree how to file, and a backbone that holds together right up until, at a precise threshold, it doesn't.

Method, in one paragraph

The network is Wikipedia's own web of citations among philosophers born before 1900, undirected and, where the week calls for it, weighted by summing the link count in both directions between a pair of articles. Four questions, four instruments: Louvain and Infomap for the disagreement in Section I; k-clique percolation for the overlap in Section II; the same Louvain run again, this time on weighted links, for Section III; and the disparity filter (Serrano, Boguñá & Vespignani, 2009) for the backbone in Section IV. Everything computes live in your browser straight from the two release TSVs; only Infomap and the k-clique scan, too heavy to repeat on every page load, are precomputed by tools/build_week4_analysis.py and just loaded here.

Section I — Two Detectives, One Case

Run Louvain and Infomap on the exact same undirected, unweighted network and they file different reports. Louvain sees a handful of broad factions; Infomap sees dozens of narrow ones. Neither is wrong — they're optimizing different things — but a reader who trusts one over the other is trusting a specific mathematical bet, and the alluvial diagram below is what that bet looks like in practice.

Section II — The Suspect with Divided Loyalties

Small k in a k-clique scan over-percolates — at k=3 and k=4, Aristotle's "community" swallows 60–85% of the entire giant component, which is not a finding, it's the failure mode the course's own notes warn about. Push k up to 6 or 7, where cliques are large enough to mean something, and a real answer appears: Aristotle sits in … separate, legible communities at once at k=7 (… at k=6) — Ancient Greek philosophy, the Islamic Golden Age, Pythagorean mathematics, and the German Idealists two thousand years later all claim a piece of him.

Section IV — The Backbone's Breaking Point

The disparity filter (Serrano, Boguñá & Vespignani, 2009) keeps a link only if it's significant at one of its two endpoints — the ones a low-degree node leans on, not just the ones a hub happens to have. Drag α down from loose to strict and watch the giant component come apart, not all at once, but branch by branch: first the philosophers connected to the rest by a single thread, then whole traditions.

Discussion

We went in chasing three of this week's suggested threads and came out with a fourth we didn't plan for. Louvain and Infomap agree enough to trust that the philosophers network really is clustered, and disagree enough that neither deserves to be cited without the other in the room. Aristotle does belong to several traditions at once — the exercise's suspicion was right — but the more interesting finding is that he isn't unusual for it; overlap tracks influence, not antiquity. Weighting the network moves a real, non-trivial slice of philosophers between factions, which is the cleanest evidence in this file that "communities" are a property of the measurement, not just the philosopher. And the backbone, which we expected to snap once, instead peels off entire traditions one at a time as α tightens — India and China first, by single threads to Voltaire and Gaudapada, and only then, deep in the strict end, does the Western canon itself start splitting into Hume's circle and Augustine's.

Three caveats. First, every method here answers to a proxy: era is a birth-century label, not a real account of who influenced whom, so the "which method tells the better history of philosophy" numbers in Section I should be read as weak evidence, not a verdict. Second, k-clique percolation is famous for being unstable in exactly the range we used it (k=6–7); we checked two values of k and both told the same story, which is reassuring but not proof against a third value telling a different one. Third, the disparity filter's "significant" edges are a property of Wikipedia's editors, not of the philosophers themselves — a bridge like Confucius–Voltaire says an editor thought it worth linking, which is real evidence of influence but not the only kind there is.
