Person of Interest

Ask who matters most in the Marvel wiki-link network and four different measures give four different answers. Ask the follow-up question — compared to what? — and most of those answers fall apart. This file is about the suspects whose answers don't.

Research question

Spider-Man tops every centrality measure we can throw at the network, so the interesting question isn't who is first. It's whether any character's position in the network is worth more than their raw number of links. A character with 106 links will sit on a lot of shortest paths no matter how the network is wired, so a big betweenness number on its own proves nothing. Which characters are genuine brokers — holding a position that a character with their link count would not normally get — and which ones are just popular?

Method, in one paragraph

We compute degree, closeness, harmonic, betweenness and PageRank on the 277-character giant component, then test every character against the null model from Case No. 02: a degree-preserving shuffle, which repeatedly picks two wiki-links and crosses their endpoints so that every character keeps their exact number of links while who-links-to-whom is scrambled. Two hundred shuffles give every character a null distribution, and the gap between their real score and that distribution — the z-score — is the only number in this file we're willing to call a finding. Everything runs in your browser from the same two TSVs as the other cases; the compute core is checked against our week 3 notebook (networkx) by tools/verify-week3.html, 89 checks, all passing.

The Interrogation Room

Pick a character and run the check. The Agency shuffles the network 200 times, keeping every character's link count exactly as it is, and asks what their betweenness would be if their position were pure chance. The histogram is that null; the bright line is the real network. Three verdicts are possible: broker (the real value sits above the null), alibi (the null explains it), and sheltered (the network routes around them). Start with Spider-Man; the evidence board below names the characters worth pulling in next.

Case Analysis II — one-way streets

Everything above treats a wiki-link as a handshake, when it is really a finger pointed one way. Spider-Man's article is linked from 106 pages and links out to nine, eight of which are other spider-characters. Put the arrows back and the network stops being a small world and starts being a set of one-way streets: roughly 69% of ordered pairs can still reach each other, the rest cannot.

Six degrees of Spider-Man

Name a character and the tracer walks the links to Spider-Man. Turn the arrows on and it walks them in both directions, which are rarely the same length.

The end of the line

Who is furthest from the middle of the universe? The answer changes completely depending on whether the arrows count.

Read that chain from left to right and every step is forced. Spider-Man's own page offers only one way out of his corner — Black Cat — because the other eight links lead to spider-characters and the Hulk. From there the trail runs through people nobody else writes about: Solo is the only page that links to G. W. Bridge, Bridge the only one that links to Garrison Kane, and Kane the only one that links to Box. Six clicks, and each of the last three has exactly one door.

Box also explains the shape of the far end. He is Alpha Flight, a Canadian team whose members mostly write about each other, hanging off the main network by a thread of mercenaries. Distance in this network is not about fame, it is about how many editors had a reason to mention you: the characters at the end of the line are the ones with a single page pointing at them.

Who gains and who loses when the arrows go back on

The same ranking as Case Analysis I, recomputed with direction. A character can only pass traffic along if their own page links out, so the pages that mention everyone climb and the pages everyone mentions fall.

There is a type at the top of that table. Betsy Braddock links out to 28 characters and is linked from 7; Doctor Nemesis, Rom the Space Knight and Deathlok are the same shape. These are long, crossover-heavy articles about characters who turn up in everyone else's stories without being the reason anyone opens a comic — useful as routes, unremarkable as destinations.

Case Analysis III — known associates

Centrality is a question about individuals. The last question in this file is about groups: not who is important, but who turns up together. A clique is the strictest version of that — a set of characters in which every single pair links to each other, with no exceptions allowed.

Team membership comes from the alliances field of each character's Wikipedia infobox, matched against eighteen well-known teams; 158 of the 277 characters in the giant component get a label that way, and the rest are shown in grey. Chip colour is the team.

So, are they teams? The biggest ones are exactly that, and they are all the same team. Every clique of eight is an X-Men line-up built from the same six regulars — Betsy Braddock, Cyclops, Emma Frost, Jean Grey, Rachel Summers and Wolverine — with two more X-Men rotating through. Wikipedia wrote the roster without meaning to.

Switch to the mixed view and a different kind of group appears, one with no roster at all. Blade, Doctor Strange, Morbius, Werewolf by Night, Ghost Rider, Man-Thing and Moon Knight are filed under four different teams, and they are mutually linked because they share a genre rather than a base: they are Marvel's horror corner, and the crossovers that pull them together are the kind the articles love to list. The largest groups in this network are rosters. The next ones down are eras, genres, and the particular corner one character lives in.

Run the alibi check in the interrogation room and this finding fills in.

Does the network prefer its own kind?

A clique is the extreme case of a milder question: do characters link to characters like themselves at all? Two labels can be read off the articles — the team a character belongs to, and the decade they first appeared in — and for each one, assortativity measures how much of the linking stays inside a label. Zero means the labels tell you nothing about who links to whom.

A number on its own says nothing here either, and the test is different from the one in the interrogation room. There we shuffled links; that would be the wrong instrument for a question about labels, since it destroys the structure under examination. Instead the network is frozen and the labels are dealt out to different characters, a thousand times. That keeps every link, every degree and the exact number of X-Men, and breaks only the tie between who a character is and where they sit.

Discussion

The Agency came into this case expecting to crown someone. What it found instead is that most of the crowning had already been done by the link count. Spider-Man sits on nearly a quarter of all shortest paths because he has 106 links, and any character with 106 links would do the same; the entire closeness ranking is fixed within a few percent before the first measurement; the network's preference for linking hubs to small characters is arithmetic, not taste. Strip out what the degrees already explain and a short list is left: a handful of genuine brokers in the middle of the table, a few characters sheltered behind a famous neighbour, and one two-link gatekeeper.

The groups are the exception, and they are the reason this file ends where it does. Cliques, teams and eras all survive the tests that flatten everything else. Whatever else the Marvel network is, it is genuinely clustered: people who appear together get written about together.

Three caveats we would want a reader to hold. First, every finding here describes Wikipedia, not the comics — Hercules bridges Asgard and the Avengers because an editor wrote those paragraphs, and Box sits at the end of the line because few editors had a reason to mention him. Second, the team labels cover 158 of 277 characters, so the team result speaks for the labelled part of the network and the grey chips are missing data, not loners. Third, two hundred shuffles put a floor under what we can claim: nothing on this page is significant beyond roughly one in two hundred, and a z-score that large should be read as "far outside", not as a precise measurement.
