Case No. 03 · Open

Person of Interest

Ask who matters most in the Marvel wiki-link network and four different measures give four different answers. Ask the follow-up question — compared to what? — and most of those answers fall apart. This file is about the suspects whose answers don't.

Research question

Spider-Man tops every centrality measure we can throw at the network, so the interesting question isn't who is first. It's whether any character's position in the network is worth more than their raw number of links. A character with 106 links will sit on a lot of shortest paths no matter how the network is wired, so a big betweenness number on its own proves nothing. Which characters are genuine brokers — holding a position that a character with their link count would not normally get — and which ones are just popular?

Method, in one paragraph

We compute degree, closeness, harmonic, betweenness and PageRank on the 277-character giant component, then test every character against the null model from Case No. 02: a degree-preserving shuffle, which repeatedly picks two wiki-links and crosses their endpoints so that every character keeps their exact number of links while who-links-to-whom is scrambled. Two hundred shuffles give every character a null distribution, and the gap between their real score and that distribution — the z-score — is the only number in this file we're willing to call a finding. Everything runs in your browser from the same two TSVs as the other cases; the compute core is checked against our week 3 notebook (networkx) by tools/verify-week3.html, 62 checks, all passing.

The Interrogation Room

Pick a character and run the check. The Agency shuffles the network 200 times, keeping every character's link count exactly as it is, and asks what their betweenness would be if their position were pure chance. The histogram is that null; the bright line is the real network. Three verdicts are possible: broker (the real value sits above the null), alibi (the null explains it), and sheltered (the network routes around them). Start with Spider-Man, then try the ones the evidence board flags later on this page.

Case Analysis II — the arrows

Everything above ignores the direction of the links, which is a convenient lie: a wiki-link points one way. Put the arrows back and only about 69% of ordered character pairs can reach each other at all. Trace a chain below, with arrows off and then on.

The default, Beta Ray Bill, is our favourite one-way street: his article mentions a brief encounter with Spider-Man, so it's one hop over. Coming back takes five, because Spider-Man's page links to only nine characters and none of them lead anywhere near Bill.

Case Analysis III — pull the thread

If brokers hold the network together, removing them should break it. We remove characters one at a time in four different orders — most betweenness first, most links first, highest PageRank first, and at random — and watch the largest surviving group shrink, all the way to 150 of the 277 characters removed. The orders are fixed in advance from the intact network rather than recomputed after each removal, which is the cheaper and more honest version of the experiment: it's what an attacker with one snapshot could actually do.

Discussion

Three weeks in, the Agency has a house rule. Case No. 01 found the hubs, Case No. 02 found out what it takes to build them, and this one is about how easily a hub's shadow gets mistaken for a finding. Every headline number here — Spider-Man's 23% of all shortest paths, the network's disassortativity, the entire closeness ranking — is what the degree sequence alone produces. The shuffle is what separates a measurement from a result, and it left us with a short list: a handful of genuine brokers in the middle of the ranking, a couple of characters whose links are worth less than they look, and one two-link gatekeeper.

One finding this week that isn't about degree: characters link to characters from their own era. Taking each character's first-appearance decade from the debut field of their Wikipedia infobox (found for 289 of the 303), the attribute assortativity is 0.079 against −0.008 ± 0.011 for a thousand shuffles of the labels across the same network — z = 7.9, and not one shuffle got there. It's a weak effect with a strong signature, and it needs a different null from everything else on this page: here we shuffle the labels and keep the links, because the question is about the labels. The catch is that the biggest hubs are mostly 1960s characters, so some of that effect is popularity wearing a costume; a stricter test would shuffle decades only among characters with similar degree.

What we'd flag for the next case. Our brokers are brokers in Wikipedia's link structure, which is a record of what editors wrote, not of what happened in the comics — Hercules bridges the Avengers, the cosmic characters and Asgard because someone wrote those paragraphs. The z-scores also assume the shuffled values are roughly normal, which is fine for betweenness in the middle of the ranking and shakier out in the tail where Rockman sits, so his z = 13 should be read as "off the scale", not as a precise number. And 200 shuffles is the floor for an empirical p-value: nothing here can be significant at better than 1 in 201.
